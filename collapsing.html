Collapsing Decision Boundaries

A plain-English walkthrough of the project — every technical term explained with real-world analogies.

🧠 What is this project about?

Imagine you're playing a quiz show. A question flashes on screen, and a countdown timer starts ticking. The closer the timer gets to zero, the more pressure you feel to just pick an answer — even if you're not sure.

This project tries to mathematically model that pressure. It asks: when time is running out, do people change how they make decisions? And if so, can we figure out the mathematical "shape" of that change by looking at how fast and how accurately they respond?

🎯 Core analogy: Think of your brain as a bank account slowly filling with "evidence" about the right answer. Once your evidence balance crosses a threshold (the "decision boundary"), you commit to an answer. This project asks: does that threshold stay fixed, or does it drop as time runs out?

🔑 The Three Big Questions

1

How do people decide under time pressure?

A study by Katsimpokis et al. (2020) found that how you create time pressure matters. Just telling someone "be fast" leads to different brain behavior than giving them a hard deadline countdown.

2

What mathematical shape does the boundary collapse in?

Previous work only tested straight-line (linear) collapse. This project tests curved shapes: an exponential drop-off or an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. Maybe those fit human behavior better.

3

Can a computer learn to figure out those shapes automatically?

Instead of doing complex math by hand, we train a neural network to learn the relationship between "behavior patterns" and "model parameters" — then use it to analyze real human data instantly.

📚 The Study it's Based On

Katsimpokis, Hawkins & van Maanen (2020) ran an experiment where people watched dots moving on a screen (some going left, some going right) and had to quickly say which direction most dots were going.

Half the time, participants were just told to be fast. The other half, a real countdown timer enforced a hard deadline.

Finding: Under verbal speed instructions → a fixed threshold model fit best. Under a hard deadline → a collapsing threshold model fit better. This project asks: can we do even better with a curved (non-linear) collapse?

⏱️ Response Time (RT) and Choices

Every time a participant makes a decision in the experiment, two things are recorded:

Response Time (RT)
measured in seconds (e.g. 0.45s)

How long it took from seeing the stimulus to pressing a button. This is the raw speed of the decision.

Choice
correct = 1 / error = 0

Was the answer right or wrong? Together with RT, this tells us about the speed-accuracy trade-off.

Example data row: Participant saw dots moving RIGHT. They pressed "right" in 0.38 seconds → RT = 0.38, Choice = 1 (correct).

🔄 Speed-Accuracy Trade-off (SAT)

This is the central tension in every decision: going faster means making more errors. You can't maximize both at the same time.

🎯 Analogy: Imagine signing your name. If someone gives you 10 seconds, it looks perfect. If someone gives you 0.5 seconds, it's a scrawl. The faster you go, the sloppier the result. Every decision in the brain works the same way.

The two SAT conditions in the experiment

Speed Instruction

Experimenter just tells the person to be fast. No timer. Psychological pressure only. → People stay cautious, threshold stays fixed.

Response Deadline

A hard cutoff enforced by the computer. After the deadline, no response is recorded. → People rush as the deadline approaches, threshold collapses.

📊 What does RT data look like?

RT distributions are always right-skewed: most responses cluster quickly, but there's a long tail of slow responses. This shape is mathematically predictable — it's one reason we use the DDM model.

Typical RT distribution — most decisions happen fast, few take long
Non-response
RT = NaN, choice = -1

If a hard deadline passes before the person responds, we record a "non-response." The model must also produce these at the same rate.

Right-skewed distribution
long tail on the right

Most RTs are fast (0.3–0.7s), but occasionally someone takes 1.5s or more. The histogram has a peak left and a long tail right.

🎯 The Diffusion Decision Model (DDM)

The DDM is the main mathematical model used to describe how people make decisions. It imagines that your brain is accumulating noisy evidence over time until it hits a threshold.

🎯 Analogy — The bank account: Imagine a bank account that starts at £0. Every millisecond, a small random amount of money is deposited or withdrawn (noise), but overall there's a slight positive drift (the signal). As soon as the balance hits +£100 (upper boundary), you decide "right!" As soon as it hits -£100 (lower boundary), you decide "left!" The time it takes to reach either limit is your Response Time.

🔑 The 4 Key Parameters

A parameter is a number that controls how the model behaves. Fitting the model means finding which numbers best explain the data.

Drift Rate (v)
v ~ how strong the signal is

How fast evidence accumulates on average. High v = easy task (fast, accurate). Low v = hard task (slow, more errors). Can be negative (evidence pulls toward the wrong answer).

Boundary Separation (a)
a ~ how cautious you are

The distance from 0 to each decision threshold. Large a = slow but accurate (careful). Small a = fast but risky. This is what collapses over time in our models.

Non-Decision Time (t₀)
t₀ ~ reaction + motor time

The time spent on things other than the actual decision — seeing the stimulus, moving the finger. Subtracted from total RT to get "pure" decision time.

Starting Point (z)
z ~ prior bias

Where evidence accumulation starts. z = 0.5 means unbiased (exactly halfway). z > 0.5 means you're already leaning toward one response before seeing anything.

Example: v=2.0, a=1.5, t₀=0.25, z=0.5 → Person is unbiased, moderately cautious, accumulating evidence quickly. Typical RT ≈ 0.5s, accuracy ≈ 85%.

🎲 Noisy Evidence — the "Wiener Process"

Evidence doesn't accumulate smoothly — it's noisy. Each tiny time step, the evidence changes by: the drift (signal) + a random jiggle (noise).

Math: dX(t) = v·dt + σ·dW(t)

In plain English: "the tiny change in evidence = (signal strength × tiny time step) + (noise × random fluctuation)"

The dW(t) part is called a Wiener process (named after mathematician Norbert Wiener). It's just a mathematical description of random jiggling — like the path of a pollen grain floating in water (Brownian motion).

Multiple simulated evidence paths — each hits a boundary at a different time (= different RT)

🧮 Euler-Maruyama: the simulation method

We can't solve the DDM equation perfectly — the math is too complex. Instead, we approximate it in tiny time steps.

1

Start at X = 0 (or starting point z)

Set a clock to 0ms and evidence to the starting value.

2

Each 1ms, add drift + random noise

X(t+1ms) = X(t) + v×0.001 + noise×√0.001

3

Check if boundary is crossed

If X ≥ +a → record RT, choice = correct. If X ≤ -a → record RT, choice = error.

4

Repeat for thousands of simulated trials

This generates a synthetic dataset of (RT, choice) pairs — just like the real experiment, but from our model.

🎯 Analogy: Like a video game that can't run physics continuously, so it recalculates positions 60 times per second instead. The smaller the time step (dt), the more accurate the simulation.

📉 Boundary Functions

The "decision boundary" is the amount of evidence needed before you commit. This project tests different mathematical shapes for how that boundary changes over time.

🎛️ Interactive: See the Boundaries

Fixed boundary: The threshold never changes. You need the same amount of evidence at t=0.1s as at t=2.0s. Best fits "speed instruction" data.

Formula: B(t) = a  |  The boundary is just the constant 'a'.

Linear collapse: The boundary drops at a constant rate — like a straight ramp down. At some point it reaches a minimum floor and stays there.

Formula: B(t) = max(a − k×t, ε)  |  'k' is the collapse rate. 'ε' is the floor so it doesn't reach zero.

Exponential collapse: The boundary drops sharply at first, then slows down (like a ball rolling off a table and slowing as it settles). It never quite reaches zero.

Formula: B(t) = a × e^(−λt)  |  'λ' (lambda) is the decay rate. Bigger λ = faster early collapse.
🎯 Analogy: Radioactive decay — a substance loses half its atoms very fast initially, then slows. The boundary does the same.

Sigmoid (S-shaped) collapse: The boundary stays high for a while, then drops steeply at a specific moment, then levels off. This models "I'm patient at first, but panic kicks in at 0.8 seconds."

Formula: B(t) = a × (1 − 1/(1 + e^(−k(t−t_mid))))  |  't_mid' is when the big drop happens.
🎯 Analogy: A game show contestant who's calm for the first 30 seconds, then suddenly panics in the last 10 seconds.

Urgency gating: Instead of lowering the boundary, this model boosts the evidence as time passes. The boundary stays fixed, but urgency adds to the accumulated evidence, making it easier to cross the threshold later.

Formula: Decision when |X(t) + u(t)| ≥ a  |  u(t) = g×t (urgency grows with time). 'g' is the urgency gain.
🎯 Analogy: Like adrenaline kicking in as a deadline approaches — it doesn't change the goal, but it amplifies your mental effort.
Fixed boundary — stays flat over time

🤖 Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)

Here's the core problem: if we change the boundary shape, we can't write a formula for the probability of any particular RT. The math just doesn't work out analytically for complex boundary shapes.

SBI is a way to bypass this problem entirely. Instead of computing the probability mathematically, we simulate millions of datasets and train a neural network to recognize patterns.

🎯 Analogy: Imagine you're trying to figure out what settings a camera was on when a photo was taken (shutter speed, aperture, ISO). You don't have a formula to compute this. But if you take 1 million photos with every setting combination and train a neural network on them, it can look at any new photo and immediately tell you the likely settings. SBI does exactly this, but for brain parameters.

📖 Key Terms in SBI

Prior Distribution
p(θ) — before seeing data

Your belief about what values the parameters could take before you see any data. "I think drift rate v is probably between -3 and +3." Encoded as a probability distribution.

Posterior Distribution
p(θ|data) — after seeing data

Your updated belief about parameters after seeing the data. "Given this person's RTs and choices, v is probably between 1.2 and 2.1." This is what we want.

Likelihood
p(data|θ) — data given params

How probable is this specific dataset if these are the true parameters? In classical methods you need this. In SBI, you don't — you just simulate instead.

Amortized Inference
train once, use forever

After training the network on millions of simulations, you can analyze any new participant's data instantly. The "cost" is paid once at training time, then amortized across all future uses.

🧠 BayesFlow — the software

BayesFlow is a Python library that implements SBI. It has two main neural network components:

Summary Network (SetTransformer)

A neural network that takes in a list of 200+ (RT, choice) pairs from one participant and compresses them into a short fixed-length "fingerprint" — a vector of 64 numbers that captures the statistical essence of that person's behavior.

🎯 Analogy: A doctor doesn't read every word in a patient's 500-page medical file. They look for patterns: "mostly normal, one spike in blood pressure, history of migraines" — a short summary. The summary network does this for RT data.

Inference Network (Normalizing Flow)

Takes that 64-number fingerprint and produces a full posterior distribution — a cloud of likely parameter values. It's a special type of neural network that learns to warp a simple random distribution (like a Gaussian) into a complex, shaped posterior.

🎯 Analogy: Think of squeezing Play-Doh through a mold. The neural network is the mold — it takes a simple blob and reshapes it into the exact shape the posterior should have.

📐 Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC)

How do you know if your trained network's posterior is trustworthy? You run SBC: a diagnostic test.

1

Pick a true θ* from the prior

Pretend you know the true parameters. E.g., v=1.5, a=1.2, t₀=0.3.

2

Simulate data with those parameters

Run the DDM simulator 200 times → get 200 (RT, choice) pairs.

3

Get posterior from BayesFlow

Feed the simulated data into the trained network → get 1000 samples of what it thinks the parameters are.

4

Check: where does θ* rank among the 1000 samples?

If the posterior is correct, θ* should land at a random rank (e.g., 273rd out of 1000) — not always at the top or bottom. Do this 1000 times: the rank distribution should be flat (uniform).

If ranks cluster at low or high values, the posterior is biased — the network is systematically under- or over-estimating parameters.

Step 1 — Define the Prior

Before looking at any data, you decide: "What are plausible values for drift rate, boundary, etc.?" This is the prior distribution.

Example: "Drift rate v is probably somewhere between -4 and +4 — let's use a Normal distribution centered at 0." This encodes the belief that most people are neither extremely fast nor extremely slow accumulators.

You also do a prior predictive check: simulate some data just from the prior and ask "does this look vaguely like real human data?" If not, your prior is too wide or too narrow.

Step 2 — Simulate Millions of Datasets

For each simulation:

a

Draw random parameters from the prior

e.g., v=0.82, a=1.34, t₀=0.21, λ=0.87

b

Run the DDM simulator 200 times with those parameters

Get 200 (RT, choice) pairs.

c

Store the pair (parameters, dataset)

Do this 100,000 times. The network will learn from all of them.

🎯 Analogy: Teaching a student to recognize handwriting by showing them 100,000 examples — each with the handwritten text AND what letter it actually is. The network learns the mapping from text to letter. BayesFlow learns the mapping from data-patterns to parameters.

Step 5 — Fit Real Data (Empirical Inference)

Load the actual data from Katsimpokis et al. (2020). For each participant in each condition:

Input: 200 rows of (RT, choice) for this participant.
→ Summary network compresses this to 64 numbers.
→ Inference network converts to 1000 parameter samples (the posterior).
Output: "Participant #12 in the deadline condition likely has v≈1.4, a≈0.9, λ≈1.8" (with uncertainty ranges).

Step 6 — Posterior Predictive Check (PPC)

The final quality check: "Does my model actually reproduce what people did?"

1

Sample 200 parameter sets from the posterior

These are the parameter values our model thinks are likely for this participant.

2

Simulate 500 trials with each parameter set

Get 200 × 500 = 100,000 synthetic (RT, choice) pairs.

3

Compare simulated vs. real RT distributions

Do the simulated RT quantiles match the real ones? Does accuracy match? If yes → good model fit.

Key plots to make:
• QP plot (Quantile-Probability): Is the 50th percentile RT in simulation ≈ real data? The 90th percentile?
• CAF (Conditional Accuracy Function): Among the fastest 20% of responses, what fraction were correct? Among the slowest 20%? Should match real data.
• Non-response rate: What fraction of trials hit the deadline? Should match.

🗺️ Glossary at a Glance

EAM

Evidence Accumulation Model — any model where decisions happen by accumulating noisy evidence until a threshold.

DDM

Diffusion Decision Model — the most popular EAM, treats evidence as a random walk with a drift.

Prior

Your beliefs about parameter values before seeing data.

Posterior

Updated beliefs after seeing data — what the model estimates the true parameters to be.

Likelihood

How probable the observed data is given specific parameters — needed in classical methods but not SBI.

SBI

Simulation-Based Inference — use simulations instead of likelihood to fit models.

BayesFlow

Python library implementing SBI with neural networks.

PPC

Posterior Predictive Check — simulate from fitted parameters and compare to real data.

SBC

Simulation-Based Calibration — diagnostic test to verify posteriors are reliable.

SAT

Speed-Accuracy Trade-off — the fundamental tension between going fast vs. being accurate.

CAF

Conditional Accuracy Function — accuracy plotted as a function of RT quantile bin.

RT

Response Time — how long from stimulus onset to button press.
